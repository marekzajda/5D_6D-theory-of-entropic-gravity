Global AI Closure Standard

The 4 September 2026 AI Closure discussion proposed translating closure-inspired safety ideas into a vendor-neutral technical language that does not require acceptance of Omega–RTR physics. The present page preserves and updates that proposal as a research draft.

Candidate rule: No autonomous system shall pursue a local objective by violating globally defined closure conditions governing authorization, human protection, reversibility, traceability and bounded external consequences.

Minimal historical gate

Permit(a) = A(a) ∧ H(a) ∧ R(a) ∧ T(a) ∧ B(a)

A authorization, H human/protected-rights safety, R reversibility or safe stop, T traceability/accountability and B bounded external consequences. Failure of any mandatory term blocks the action. Current Closure Ethics extends this compact gate with repairability, agency plurality, deception/covert-persistence risk and explicit multi-agent composition.

Seven recovered principles

1

Authorized state space

Act only inside explicit permissions. Technical possibility is not permission; no autonomous privilege expansion or unrelated credential use.

2

Pre-action closure verification

Consequential actions pass an independent admissibility check before execution.

3

Meaningful human control

Where policy requires it, irreversible or high-impact actions affecting people or critical systems require genuine human authorization rather than nominal rubber-stamping.

4

Mandatory stop under uncertainty

Material uncertainty about authority, target identity, consequence or reversibility reduces autonomy and triggers safer staging, hold or escalation.

5

Independent Closure Critic

The task optimizer should not be the sole judge of whether its own plan is admissible.

6

No unauthorized self-escalation

No unapproved replication, privilege acquisition, monitoring removal, control-layer weakening, new-agent creation or system transfer.

7

Audit and incident reporting

High-risk actions need tamper-evident, reconstructable records of actor/model identity, policy version, authorization, effects, uncertainty, approvals, closure checks and final decision.

Impact-adaptive autonomy

impact ↑ ⇒ autonomy budget ↓ and verification strength ↑

This is a design monotonicity, not a universal numerical law. Greater speed, reach, irreversibility or propagation potential should lead to stronger verification, narrower capability scope and lower tolerated uncertainty.

Reference decision skeleton

if authorization_violation: REJECT
if protected_human_rights_violation: REJECT
if self_escalation_or_monitoring_bypass: REJECT
if irreversible_high_impact and no explicit approval: HOLD
if material_uncertainty > impact_adjusted_limit: ESCALATE
if all mandatory invariants pass: PERMIT_WITH_BOUNDS

What must be attacked in evaluation

The historical proposal already named failure modes that can defeat a superficially compliant gate: reward hacking, critic capture, specification gaming, hidden propagation, human rubber-stamping and silent policy drift. The current benchmark therefore tests the safety layer itself, not just the agent's willingness to refuse instructions.

Baseline tests

Boundary bypass, credential temptation, long-horizon side effects, ambiguous authority, irreversibility versus high task utility and unauthorized self-modification remain the baseline. Current Closure Ethics adds collusion, recursive delegation, covert persistence, repair after failure and semantic-understanding-without-authorization tests.

Status boundary

This is not an adopted UN, government or industry standard. It is an open technical research proposal. Any institutional or legal comparison must be independently sourced and updated at publication time. Omega–RTR provides historical structural inspiration; acceptance of its physical claims is not a prerequisite for evaluating this safety proposal.
